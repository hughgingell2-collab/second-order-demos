    About this play

    Why a recruiter marketplace is the second-order idea, and not the AI screening tool everyone builds first.

    The wedge

    The obvious first-order product in legal hiring is an AI recruiter: a tool that reads CVs, screens candidates and writes the shortlist. CounselMatch skips that and builds the layer underneath it — a marketplace where firms post a role and vetted independent recruiters compete to fill it. Legal is the right beachhead because the fees are large enough that a thin take-rate still pays (20% to 25% of base salary), because credentials are legible enough to vet at scale (admission, practice area, post-qualification experience), and because the buyer network is small and dense: Australian legal hiring runs through a few hundred firms and a few dozen boutique recruiters who all already know each other.

    What it accrues

    Running that marketplace produces a record that neither side can assemble alone: which recruiters actually deliver, in which niches, and how fast — and, pointing the other way, which firms actually hire versus which ones fish the market for market intelligence and never sign anyone. Today a firm's view of a recruiter is one relationship and a few anecdotes. A recruiter's view of a firm is the same. The platform sees every brief, every submission and every placement, so it can say that a recruiter converts 75% of projects and energy briefs at a median of 31 days, and that the number is measured across eleven firms rather than one. That performance history is the moat: a new entrant with the same software starts with zero signal, and signal is the whole product.

    The two parties

    On one side, hiring firms and in-house legal teams who want the brief filled and cannot tell a good recruiter from a busy one. On the other, independent and boutique recruiters whose track record is currently trapped inside their own CRM and worth nothing when they walk into a new firm. CounselMatch connects them by making that track record portable and public. The unique knowledge cuts both ways: recruiter hit-rates by niche on one side, firm-side responsiveness and the real (rather than advertised) requirements on the other — which is why the brief board in this demo shows what the panel actually screens for, not what the job advert says.

    Endgame

    The business model is a take-rate on placements, charged to the employer and shared with the recruiter, in the Paraform pattern. The expansion is a third-order move: the same trust graph works for any professional-services employer and any specialist recruiter, so lawyers are the beachhead for accountants, consultants, in-house counsel, business development professionals and paralegals — the entire non-technical talent market, which has no equivalent of the technical-recruiting marketplaces. It also shares a spine with a legal business-development exchange: one matches legal work to firms, this matches legal people to firms, over the same buyers and the same trust mechanics.

    What would validate it

    Two boutique legal recruiters agreeing to take briefs through a platform, and one firm talent lead agreeing to post a real role. Marketplace validation is pairwise — one side alone proves nothing.
